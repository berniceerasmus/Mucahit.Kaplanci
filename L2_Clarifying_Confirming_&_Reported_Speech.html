Clarifying, Confirming & Reported Speech

Business English — Phase 1, Lesson 2 · Reported Speech & Indirect Questions

This lesson covers how to report what someone said and ask questions more politely — useful both in business settings (reporting back a client's feedback, confirming meeting details) and everyday conversation (retelling a story, asking a stranger for information). The Lesson tab covers the grammar, vocabulary, and in-session practice; the Homework tab reinforces it with a longer reading passage and extra practice, including a writing + voice note task.
Business Insight

When you report back what a client or colleague said — in an email, a meeting recap, or casual conversation — small reporting errors (tense, word order) can quietly change the meaning. "She said she was happy" and "She says she's happy" carry different implications about timing and current feelings. Getting reported speech right keeps your summaries accurate, in business and in daily life alike.

1. Grammar — Reported Speech & Indirect Questions ▶

Reported statements: the verb usually moves one step back in time.
"I'm happy with the offer." → She said (that) she was happy with the offer.
"We will deliver on Friday." → They said (that) they would deliver on Friday.

Reported questions: word order becomes statement order, and the question mark disappears.
"Where is the invoice?" → He asked where the invoice was.
"Did you send the contract?" → She asked if/whether I had sent the contract.

Indirect questions make a direct question sound more polite, often used with strangers or senior colleagues: Could you tell me what time the meeting starts? instead of What time does the meeting start?

2. Vocabulary — Clarifying & Following Up ▶
  • to paraphrase — to repeat something in different words
  • to clarify — to make something clearer or less confusing
  • to recap — to briefly summarize what was said
  • to follow up — to continue or check on something later
  • to get back to someone — to reply or respond at a later time
  • to touch base — to make brief contact to check on progress
  • to loop someone in — to include someone in a conversation or decision
  • to flag something — to point out an issue so it gets attention
  • to circle back — to return to a topic later in a discussion
  • to iron out (details) — to resolve small remaining problems

6. Reading & Discussion ▶

A miscommunication once cost a sales team almost a week of lost progress. During a call with a distributor in South Africa, the distributor said he was "fairly confident" the board would approve the new contract terms, but added that he would need to confirm by email. The sales rep who took the call reported back to the team that the deal was "basically done." Based on that recap, the operations team began preparing shipment documents and blocking warehouse space for the order. Three days later, the distributor's email arrived — the board had, in fact, asked for a lower minimum order quantity before approving anything. The operations team had to undo several days of preparation, and the sales rep had to explain to the regional director what had gone wrong. Afterward, the team introduced a simple rule: any recap of a call involving a decision had to include the exact words used, not just the reporter's impression of what they meant — so "fairly confident, pending board approval" would be reported as exactly that, rather than rounded up to "done." The rule seemed small, but it prevented at least two similar mix-ups in the following months, and the team began treating accurate reporting as seriously as the negotiation itself.
