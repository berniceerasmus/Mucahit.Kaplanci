Framing Recommendations & Diplomatic Disagreement

Business English — Phase 1, Lesson 1 · Modals of Speculation & Hedging Language

This lesson focuses on softening strong opinions and disagreeing diplomatically with international colleagues and clients — using modals of speculation (might, could, may well) and hedging phrases (I'd suggest, it might be worth, I wonder if). The Lesson tab covers the grammar, vocabulary, and in-session practice; the Homework tab reinforces it with a longer reading passage and extra practice, including a writing + voice note task.
Business Insight

In many international sales and client-facing roles, directness can be read as confidence in some cultures and as rudeness in others. Managers who can soften a recommendation without weakening it — "This approach might work better" instead of "Your approach is wrong" — build trust faster across offices and close more deals with international clients.

1. Grammar — Modals of Speculation & Hedging ▶

Modals of speculation let you express how certain you are about something:

might / could / may — possible, not certain ("This might delay the shipment.")
may well — fairly likely ("The client may well ask for a discount.")
must — near certain, based on evidence ("Sales must have dropped — the numbers are down.")
can't — near certain something is not true ("That can't be right — check the invoice again.")

Hedging phrases soften an opinion or recommendation: I'd suggest…, it might be worth…, perhaps we could…, I wonder if…, it's possible that…, you may want to consider….

2. Vocabulary — Diplomacy & Negotiation Language ▶
  • play devil's advocate — argue the opposite side to test an idea
  • table an idea — put an idea forward for discussion
  • push back — resist or disagree with a proposal
  • find common ground — identify shared interests between two sides
  • read between the lines — understand a meaning that isn't stated directly
  • sugarcoat — make bad news sound better than it is
  • tactful — careful not to upset or offend someone
  • candid — honest and direct, sometimes bluntly so
  • reservations — doubts or concerns about a plan
  • reach a consensus — arrive at general agreement among a group

6. Reading & Discussion ▶

When the İstanbul sales team began working more closely with the company's new office in Dubai, early calls were tense. The İstanbul team was used to a direct style — raising problems plainly and expecting quick decisions. The Dubai team, working with clients across several Gulf countries, tended to build relationships slowly and preferred indirect, carefully hedged language, especially when disagreeing. Several early misunderstandings arose: a direct comment from İstanbul about a delayed shipment was interpreted as an accusation, while a softly-worded concern from Dubai about pricing was initially missed altogether because it didn't sound urgent enough. After a joint training session on cross-cultural communication, both teams agreed on a shared approach: raise concerns early, but frame them with hedging language such as "it might be worth" or "I wonder if," and always follow up important points in writing to avoid ambiguity. Within two months, the number of miscommunications dropped sharply, and the two offices began closing joint deals with international distributors far more smoothly. The sales manager overseeing both teams noted that the biggest shift wasn't in language ability at all — it was in learning to read intent behind tone, and in trusting that a softly-phrased concern from a colleague could be just as serious as a direct one. Team members began checking in with each other more often outside of formal calls, which also helped build the kind of trust that made future disagreements easier to raise without tension.
